A computer can look at a photo and recognize a cat, listen to speech and write down the words, or translate a sentence from one language into another. To do these things, we do not manually describe the shape of a cat's ears, explain how to separate every sound, or write down every rule of translation. The computer learns useful patterns from examples. Deep learning is an approach that uses neural networks with many layers to learn such patterns from data. But what does that definition mean in practice? In traditional programming, a person writes rules and the computer follows them. In deep learning, we give a model many examples together with their correct answers. Suppose the task is to classify a photo as a cat or a dog. During training, the model examines an image, makes a prediction, and compares that prediction with the correct label. The model is not simply memorizing a ready-made rule. It learns to represent recurring visual patterns in the data as numbers. That is why it can also make a prediction for a new photo it has never seen before. Think of a neural network as layers of connected computational units. The first layer receives the raw input. For an image, that input might be its pixel values. Early layers may detect low-level features such as edges and simple textures. Later layers can combine them into richer representations related to curves, eyes, ears, and eventually an entire object. We do not specify in advance exactly which feature each layer must learn. Training gradually builds representations that are useful for the task. This is called feature learning. The final layer uses the learned features to produce an output. Here, the probability of cat is high and the probability of dog is low. At first, the network's internal parameters, its weights and biases, are nearly random. Its initial predictions are therefore usually poor. A loss function produces a number that measures how far the prediction is from the correct answer. A large loss means the model still has a lot to improve. Backpropagation calculates gradients that show how much each parameter contributed to the error. An optimizer then updates those parameters by taking a small step in a direction that reduces the loss. As the cycle of prediction, measurement, and updating repeats across thousands or millions of examples, the network's predictions gradually improve. In deep learning, this adjustment of parameters is the learning. So, where does the word deep come from? A network with many hidden layers between its input and output is called a deep neural network. Its depth allows the model to build representations in many stages. The output of one stage becomes the input to the next. This allows a hierarchy of complex patterns to grow from simpler ones. Pixels become edges, edges become parts, and parts become an object. But adding more layers does not create intelligence by itself. Good performance also requires enough data, a suitable architecture, computing power, and the right training process. Deep learning is powerful, but it is not magic. A model can learn biased patterns from weak or biased data. It can fail on unfamiliar inputs, and even a confident prediction can be wrong. Remember the core idea. Take data from examples, build useful representations through layers, and improve the parameters using the error. At scale, this process makes complex tasks, such as image recognition, speech, and language possible. In the next video, we will clearly distinguish machine learning from deep learning.
